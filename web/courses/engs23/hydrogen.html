    The physics

    Where the lifetimes come from

    An excited atom is coupled to the electromagnetic field even in the dark. Fermi's golden rule with the free-space density of photon modes, ρ(ω) = ω²V/(π²c³), summed over both polarizations and every direction, gives the Einstein A coefficient (Griffiths §11.3)  A = ω³|⟨b|qr|a⟩|²/(3πε0ħc³). The page evaluates the dipole matrix element exactly: the angular part with the Ylm (it vanishes unless Δl = ±1 and Δm = 0, ±1, the selection rules), the radial part ∫RnlRn′l′r³dr numerically. Adding every downward channel gives the lifetime, τ = 1/ΣA: 1.60 ns for 2p, 158 ns for 3s. The ω³ is why ultraviolet transitions are fast and infrared ones slow.

    Why 2s lives 0.12 seconds

    The only state below 2s is 1s, and Δl = 0 is forbidden for a single photon, so 2s cannot decay the way 2p does. It decays by emitting two photons at once (a second-order process, 8.229 s⁻¹: Spitzer & Greenstein; Goldman & Drake), which this page quotes rather than computes. That makes 2s metastable: atoms can be parked there and probed with visible light on the Balmer lines.

    Line shapes: natural, power and Doppler width

    A state that lives τ has an energy spread ħ/τ, so the line is a Lorentzian of full width Γ/2π = 1/(2πτ), 99.7 MHz for Lyman-α. Strong light broadens it by √(1 + s), with s = I/Isat and Isat = ħ²Γ²cε0/(4d²). Moving atoms see the laser Doppler shifted, which smears the line into a Gaussian of FWHM ν0√(8kBT ln2/mc²): about 30 GHz for Lyman-α at room temperature, 300 times the natural width. Only below about 3 mK does the natural width show (Foot, ch. 7–8). The page combines the two with a pseudo-Voigt profile (Thompson, Cox & Hastings).

    What this model leaves out

    Fine structure, the Lamb shift and hyperfine structure (they split and shift these lines by ~10⁻⁵ of their wavelength, e.g. Lyman-α is really two lines at 121.5668 and 121.5673 nm); photoionization above 13.6 eV; optical pumping (an atom driven on 1s→3p falls to 2s 11.8% of the time and stays there, so a real experiment slowly empties the ground state); and the two-level Bloch equations treat each line on its own. Real Lyman-α light is vacuum ultraviolet: air absorbs it, so these experiments run in vacuum with frequency-tripled or -quadrupled lasers (Eikema, Walz & Hänsch 2001). The record-precise 1s→2s measurement instead uses two 243 nm photons (Parthey et al. 2011), a two-photon transition this page does not simulate.
