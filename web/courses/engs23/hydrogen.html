How the lab link works

Decay board · a quantum Galton board

A π pulse lifts atoms from 1s to the state you pick. Then let go. Each atom waits a random time on its level (the lifetime is the average wait), then drops to a lower level and emits one photon. Which level it drops to is random too, weighted by the branching ratios A/ΣA from the dipole matrix elements. Every atom ends in 1s and stays there, so no atom is ever lost: atoms dropped = atoms in 1s + atoms still on the way. The tally at the bottom counts the route each atom took, and the dashed outlines show the predicted share of each route.

Where is the bell curve?

In Galton's board every ball meets many pegs and goes left or right with probability ½ at each one; its slot counts how often it went right, so the slots fill as a binomial distribution, which for many rows is a bell curve (the central limit theorem). Here each level is a “peg” with several exits of unequal weight, and an atom passes only a few of them, so the slots fill to the branching-ratio products instead: from 3p, 88% of atoms go straight home to 1s. That is not a bell curve, and the page does not pretend it is.

Bell curves do appear in two honest places. (1) Repeat the experiment: the number of atoms that land in one slot out of N is binomial, with mean Np and spread √(Np(1 − p)), so over many runs it scatters in a bell curve; that spread is why the bars only approach the outlines as 1/√N. (2) Add up random waits: the time a long cascade takes is a sum of independent exponential waits, which already looks peaked for 7i → 6h → … → 1s (six steps; pick it under “Start in any state”, then open “How long the cascade takes”).

The randomness is quantum. Identically prepared atoms take different routes at different times, and nothing in the initial state decides which; quantum mechanics gives only the probabilities (Einstein noticed in 1917 that his A coefficients leave the moment and direction of emission to “chance”).

Start in any state

One photon from 1s reaches only np states (Δl = ±1). Other states can be made with several photons, electron collisions or recombination in a plasma. High-l “circular” states such as 5g or 7i can only step down one level at a time (Δl = −1 each step), so their route is fixed.

What the board leaves out

The levels are shown evenly spaced by n, not to energy scale. The jumps themselves are instantaneous; they are drawn slowed so you can follow them. Lifetimes and branching ratios are the page's own gross-structure values (no fine structure), which agree with the tabulated ones (Wiese & Fuhr 2009) to better than 0.1%. The 2s atoms are free, in zero field: a modest electric field (a few hundred V/cm) mixes 2s with 2p and empties it within tens of nanoseconds (quenching), and collisions do the same in a dense gas. The 2s two-photon decay shares its energy between the two photons in a continuous spectrum, so it adds no line.

Drive and decay · you steer the laser, nature rolls the dice

The idea: a laser drives a transition. It moves an atom smoothly up and back down (Rabi flopping), as hard and as often as you like. Decay is not yours to steer. Every excited level empties at a fixed rate set by Fermi's golden rule, only into the levels the selection rules allow (Δl = ±1), and chance decides which atom goes when. Keep driving one transition and watch decay shuffle the atoms into other levels.

Fermi's golden rule: why decay is a rate and driving is not

A state coupled weakly to a continuum of final states leaks into it at a constant rate,  Γi→f = (2π/ħ) |⟨f|H′|i⟩|² ρ(Ef) (Fermi's golden rule; Griffiths §11.3–11.4). For spontaneous emission the continuum is the photon modes of empty space, with density ρ(ω) ∝ ω², and the coupling is the electric dipole H′ = −qE·r with the vacuum field (∝ √ω). Together they give the Einstein A coefficient, ∝ ω³|⟨f|r|i⟩|². Three consequences you can see here:

  1. A constant rate means a memoryless decay. The chance to jump in the next nanosecond does not depend on how long the atom has waited, so waiting times are exponential and the Monte Carlo atoms jump at random.
  2. The matrix element sets the selection rules. ⟨f|r|i⟩ vanishes unless Δl = ±1 (and Δm = 0, ±1), so each level has only a few exits, with branching ratios A/ΣA. The same matrix element decides what a laser can drive.
  3. The ω³ makes ultraviolet decays fast (2p: 1.6 ns) and infrared ones slow.

A laser is the opposite case: one mode, strongly occupied, coherent. It does not empty a level at a rate; it rotates the atom between two states, Pe = sin²(Ωt/2), which grows as t² at first, not as t. With strong driving you see that flopping in the first few nanoseconds before decay scrambles it. A rate description of absorption (Einstein's B coefficient, again the golden rule) returns only for broadband light, or once decay has destroyed the coherence.

Monte Carlo and probabilities: two views of one equation

📊 Probabilities solve the master equation: the optical Bloch equations for the driven pair (populations ρgg, ρee and the coherence ρeg), plus a rate equation for every other level, dPk/dt = −ΓkPk + ΣAj→kPj. This is what a large cloud of atoms does on average, and it is smooth and deterministic.

🎲 Monte Carlo atoms follow single atoms (the Monte Carlo wavefunction method, Dalibard, Castin & Mølmer 1992). While an atom is in the driven pair it evolves smoothly (it rides up and down the laser arrow), slowly losing norm at the rate Γe|ce|²; when a random threshold is crossed it makes a quantum jump, emitting one photon to a level picked by the branching ratios. Levels outside the laser simply wait an exponential time and jump. Average many atoms and you get the probabilities back: the dots on the plot scatter around the lines by about √(P(1 − P)/N), which is why 1000 atoms look smoother than 100. This is what a single trapped atom or ion does, photon by photon; its fluorescence switches on and off as it falls into and out of dark states.

What this leaves out

The laser sits exactly on resonance and the atoms do not move (no Doppler shift). Levels are gross structure (no fine or hyperfine structure), drawn evenly spaced by n, not to energy scale. Linearly polarized light drives only the upper level's m = 0 sublevel; atoms that cascade into that level from above arrive unpolarized, so a third of them land in m = 0 and are driven, and the rest (hollow dots) just decay. Photons scattered back into the laser beam by stimulated emission are not counted as emitted photons. 2s decays by two photons (τ = 0.12 s), far slower than anything shown, so on these time scales it is a trap; the laser could also ionize atoms or mix in nearby levels at high power, which is ignored.

    The physics

    Where the lifetimes come from

    An excited atom is coupled to the electromagnetic field even in the dark. Fermi's golden rule with the free-space density of photon modes, ρ(ω) = ω²V/(π²c³), summed over both polarizations and every direction, gives the Einstein A coefficient (Griffiths §11.3; the golden-rule derivation is §11.4)  A = ω³|⟨b|qr|a⟩|²/(3πε0ħc³). The page evaluates the dipole matrix element exactly: the angular part with the Ylm (it vanishes unless Δl = ±1 and Δm = 0, ±1, the selection rules), the radial part ∫RnlRn′l′r³dr numerically. Adding every downward channel gives the lifetime, τ = 1/ΣA: 1.60 ns for 2p, 158 ns for 3s. The ω³ is why ultraviolet transitions are fast and infrared ones slow.

    Why 2s lives 0.12 seconds

    The only state below 2s is 1s, and Δl = 0 is forbidden for a single photon, so 2s cannot decay the way 2p does. It decays by emitting two photons at once (a second-order process, 8.229 s⁻¹ from Goldman & Drake; Spitzer & Greenstein first found 8.23 s⁻¹), which this page quotes rather than computes. That makes 2s metastable: atoms can be parked there and probed with visible light on the Balmer lines.

    Line shapes: natural, power and Doppler width

    A state that lives τ has an energy spread ħ/τ, so the line is a Lorentzian of full width Γ/2π = 1/(2πτ), 99.7 MHz for Lyman-α. Strong light broadens it by √(1 + s), with s = I/Isat and Isat = ħ²Γ²cε0/(4d²). Moving atoms see the laser Doppler shifted, which smears the line into a Gaussian of FWHM ν0√(8kBT ln2/mc²): about 30 GHz for Lyman-α at room temperature, 300 times the natural width. Only below about 3 mK does the natural width show (Foot, ch. 7–8). The page combines the two with a pseudo-Voigt profile (Thompson, Cox & Hastings).

    What this model leaves out

    Fine structure, the Lamb shift and hyperfine structure (they split and shift these lines by ~10⁻⁵ of their wavelength, e.g. Lyman-α is really two lines at 121.5668 and 121.5674 nm, while for He⁺ and Li²⁺ the gross-structure wavelengths shown here sit ~0.002 nm from the measured lines, because fine structure grows as Z⁴); photoionization above the ionization energy; optical pumping, which matters most for the 2s start: an atom driven from 2s to np falls back to 2s only ~12% of the time (3p → 1s is 88%), so after about 8 scattered photons it has left 2s for good, and the Balmer steady state, saturation and flopping shown here are an idealised closed system (in the other direction, an atom driven on 1s→3p falls to 2s 11.8% of the time and stays there, so a real experiment slowly empties the ground state); and the two-level Bloch equations treat each line on its own. Real Lyman-α light is vacuum ultraviolet, so these experiments run in vacuum, with light made by nonlinear frequency mixing (four-wave mixing in mercury vapour for a continuous source: Eikema, Walz & Hänsch 2001). The record-precise 1s→2s measurement instead uses two 243 nm photons (Parthey et al. 2011), a two-photon transition this page does not simulate.
