Pickup signal — this is what you hear

coil senses

Each string's contribution (its colour) and the clipped output Vout(t) you hear (dark), in volts. Raise the Amp gain (dB) to drive it into the ±4.5 V rail.

A magnetic pickup's voltage is the induced EMF, −dΦ/dt, so it follows the string's velocity at the coil, not its position. Differentiating in time multiplies mode n by its frequency, so higher harmonics come out n times stronger than in the position picture: that is why an electric guitar sounds bright. Switch to position to compare. The gain knob drives the sum through a soft clipper (tanh); the dark sum trace is exactly the sound. Click a name in the legend to hide that string.

Each string's FFT is one trace, with the superimposed total overlaid. Click a name in the legend to isolate a string. When clean the per-string spectra add to the total; turn the gain up and the total grows extra harmonics the strings never had — clipping manufactures frequencies, which is how a guitar amp makes distortion.

Exercise — find the Fourier transform hiding in the pickup
  1. Mute every string but the open low E (click the gutter ×). Set Pure mode → 1, press Set, then ♪ Listen. One mode on the string, one line in the FFT, one pure tone. Note its frequency f₁.
  2. Step the pure mode to 2, 3, 4 … Each single standing wave gives one spectral line at n·f₁. A sinusoid in time transforms to a single spike — the simplest Fourier transform there is.
  3. Keep that single mode and slowly raise the Amp gain (dB). The round sine in the scope grows flat tops as it hits the ±4.5 V rail. In the FFT, new peaks appear at 3f₁, 5f₁, 7f₁ … Listen: the pure tone turns into a buzzy overdrive. Where did those frequencies come from if the string only has one mode?
  4. At 0 dB the FFT peaks track the mode bars (except modes with a node at the coil), multiplied by n for the velocity-sensing coil; switch the coil to position and they match the bars directly. Now crank the gain — the FFT sprouts harmonics the string never had. Clipping is a nonlinear operation; it manufactures frequencies, which is exactly how a guitar amp makes distortion.
  5. Read it off quantitatively: the labelled peaks are at integer multiples of f₁ (in Hz). Odd multiples dominate for symmetric clipping — predict the next peak's frequency before you look.
  6. Fret a second string and press Play. Each string adds its own comb of lines in its colour; the sound and its FFT are the superposition. You have built a chord's spectrum from scratch.

Same skeleton as heat and Schrödinger: separate variables → eigenfunctions from the spatial problem + boundary conditions → each mode evolves by its eigenvalue. Here the eigenvalue is real-and-imaginary-free in space but gives ±iω in time, so modes oscillate instead of decay.
