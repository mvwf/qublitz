The Drum Head

A circular membrane of radius a is clamped to a rigid rim, like a drum head, and its displacement z(r, θ, t) obeys the wave equation (Boas, ch. 13 §6). Separating variables in polar coordinates gives Bessel functions in r, and the clamped rim allows only the zeros kmn of Jn. Pick normal modes and watch them oscillate side by side on one clock, or strike the head and see which modes it rings.

Laid out as Boas's Figure 6.1: m across, n down. Each tile is Jn(kmnr/a) cos nθ, drawn still at a crest (each one would oscillate at its own frequency; the drum and the panel below show that motion). It has n nodal diameters and m − 1 nodal circles inside the rim. The number is νmn/ν10. Every mode with n ≥ 1 has a twin, sin nθ, turned by 90°/n, with the same frequency. After a strike each tile shows its mode's share of the energy (the cos and sin twins together). The bars set how much of each selected mode is in the superposition, from −1 to +1 (its peak height); the drum, the pickup, the FFT, the sound and the spectrogram all follow.

Each drum is one normal mode, started at t = 0 at the height set by its amplitude bar. The dial turns once per oscillation, so a mode with νmn = 1.59 ν10 turns 1.59 times while the fundamental turns once. Below, the time factor cos(2πνmnt) of each mode over the last few fundamental periods T₁₀ = 1/ν10; dots mark each mode's crests. On a string, harmonic n makes exactly n oscillations per T₁, so every harmonic is back at a crest on every grid line and the string repeats each period. Here only the fundamental is. Hear two modes plays exactly the pair drawn in the first two traces, (1,0) and (1,1), as a steady two-partial tone at the drum's ratio 1 : 1.593, and then the same two partials retuned to the string's 1 : 2. At 1 : 2 the sum repeats every T₁₀ and the ear fuses it into one note an octave rich; at 1 : 1.593 it never repeats (the crest dots drift) and you hear two pitches that do not settle, a beating roughness.

Pickup signal: this is what you hear

pickup at the centre

The head's displacement (or velocity) at the pickup, over the last four fundamental periods of the animation clock, with time in milliseconds for the drum set below. The bold curve is the sum; each mode's own contribution is drawn faintly in its colour (with Your superposition). Mode (m,n) reaches the pickup with weight Jn(kmnrp/a) cos nθp, so at the centre, where Jn(0) = 0 for every n ≥ 1, only the n = 0 modes are heard.

FFT of the pickup signal

frequency components

The Fourier transform of the pickup signal (64 fundamental periods, Hann window), in units of the signal. Each peak is one normal mode, at νmn = kmnv/(2πa); the dotted lines are the string's harmonics nν10 for comparison. A selected mode the pickup cannot hear is marked ✕. Move the pickup off the centre and the n ≥ 1 peaks appear. The dashed line is the mean frequency ⟨f⟩ = Σ νmnAmn² / Σ Amn², each mode weighted by its power at the pickup: where the sound's energy sits on average, which sets how bright it sounds.

Live spectrogram of the pickup

inferno: dark = quiet, yellow = loud

The same short-time FFT as the Spectrogram Explorer. Without sound it runs silently on the pickup signal of the ideal membrane, so every mode the pickup hears is a steady horizontal line: strike the head, pick modes or move the pickup and watch the lines change. Press ♪ and it shows the sound instead (the last 8192 samples per column), where the lines fade because the sound has a decay added for listening; the ideal membrane rings forever. The ticks on the right and the dashed lines below are the predicted νmn. The dashed white line is the mean frequency ⟨f⟩. Slide the upper limit up to see the high modes a sharp strike or an off-centre pickup brings in (it is set in multiples of ν10, so it follows the drum's size and tension; the history clears when you change it). Wave speed v = √(T/σ) = 87.7 m/s, fundamental ν10 = k10v/(2πa) = 189 Hz.

The defaults are a 14-inch tom with a 0.19 mm Mylar head (σ ≈ 0.26 kg/m²). ♪ Drum plays the pickup signal: every mode the pickup can hear, at its νmn and with the amplitude it has at the pickup. ♪ Same partials, made harmonic keeps those loudnesses but moves the j-th lowest partial to jν10, as a string would have it. The ideal membrane of (6.1) never stops ringing; the sound alone gets a decay so it can end. A real drum's air loading and stiff head shift these ratios (timpani are tuned to make a few of them nearly harmonic); (6.1) leaves both out.

Exercise — the drum is not a string
  1. Press Lowest four. The first dial is the fundamental (1,0). How many turns has the (1,1) dial made when the fundamental has made exactly one? Check it against k11/k10 in the table.
  2. Press Start them together and watch the traces. Do the four modes ever all reach a crest at the same instant again? What would have to be true of the ratios for that to happen? Then press ♪ (1,0) + (1,1) and ♪ retuned to 1 : 2 under the traces: which pair sounds like one note, and which never settles?
  3. Select (2,0) alone, turn on sand in the Top view, and measure the radius of the nodal circle. Boas predicts r = a k10/k20. Why is that the right radius?
  4. Switch to Mallet strike and strike the exact centre. Which tiles light up, and why can a centre strike never excite a mode with n ≥ 1? Then strike near the rim and compare the tiles and the sound.
  5. Strike once, then play ♪ Drum and ♪ Same partials, made harmonic. Same loudnesses, different frequencies: which one has a pitch you could sing?
Built for ENGS 23 · Systems, Fields & Waves, following Boas, Mathematical Methods in the Physical Sciences, ch. 13 §6. Same skeleton as the string: separate variables → eigenfunctions from the spatial problem and the boundary condition → each mode oscillates at its own eigenvalue. In polar coordinates the eigenfunctions are Bessel functions and the eigenvalues are their zeros, which are not evenly spaced. Bessel functions are computed from Bessel's integral and the zeros by bracketing and Newton's method; tests/drum-engine.test.js checks them against Abramowitz & Stegun.
