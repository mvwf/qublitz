A rotation keeps its own axis

Turning about x leaves ⟨σx⟩ alone and trades ⟨σy⟩ against ⟨σz⟩: watch the strip of the axis you hold go flat. On the sphere the state rides the dotted circle around the glowing axis.

X and Y move P(1); Z does not

X and Y are the in-phase and quadrature parts of one microwave drive, so they swing the state between |0⟩ and |1⟩. Z only turns the phase φ around the equator; P(1) = (1 − ⟨σz⟩)/2 stays put.

Gates are just turns

A π pulse (half turn) about x is the X gate; a π/2 pulse is √X. About z, π/2 is S and π/4 is T. The readout names the gate when your turn lands on one.

The physics

In the frame rotating with a drive that is exactly on resonance, the qubit's Hamiltonian has no σz term of its own. Adding one is up to you:

H = (ħΩ/2)(ax σx + ay σy + az σz),  dr/dt = ω × r,  ω = Ω (ax, ay, az)

so the Bloch vector r = (⟨σx⟩, ⟨σy⟩, ⟨σz⟩) stays put unless driven, and turns about ω at rate |ω| while you drive (here Ω = 1.8 rad/s, so a π pulse takes 1.75 s; a real transmon does it in tens of nanoseconds). The X and Y controls are the two quadratures of the microwave drive: its phase α picks the equatorial axis (cos α, sin α, 0). The Z control detunes the drive by Ω while you hold it, which in the rotating frame is a precession about z, and stops when you let go.

Because a rotation about n keeps r·n fixed, holding X freezes ⟨σx⟩ and sends ⟨σy⟩ and ⟨σz⟩ around a circle: from |0⟩ that is the Rabi oscillation, P(1) = sin²(Ωt/2). A Z rotation never changes ⟨σz⟩, so it is invisible to a measurement in the |0⟩/|1⟩ basis, yet it changes the state: a later X or Y pulse takes it somewhere else.

In the Virtual Qubit Lab your moves become square pulses on the σx and σy channels of a 5 GHz qubit with ΩR/2π = 50 MHz (a π pulse is 10 ns) and long T₁, so the lab's P(1) matches the game. The lab has no σz channel, so Z turns become a virtual Z, as on real hardware: every later pulse has its phase shifted instead, which gives the same P(1); the final state differs only by a leftover turn about z. Lower T₁ there to watch the same moves decohere. (The lab keeps the terms the rotating-wave approximation drops, so with a much faster drive its answer would drift by a few degrees.)

Point to steer (Targets and Circuit) uses the same fact: a rotation about an equatorial axis n joins r to a point p in one turn when (r − p)·n = 0, that is n ∥ (r − p) × z, a real choice of drive phase. Circuit targets are the states a random gate sequence (H, S, T, X, Y, Z, √X, √Y, Rx(π/4), Ry(π/3)) makes from |0⟩; the dotted arc is the path the gate's own rotation would take.
