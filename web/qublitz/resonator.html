What to look for

    Real-world extras

    A real line scales and rotates the signal, and its delay makes the phase wind with frequency, so the circle becomes a spiral. Fitting removes these first. φ makes the dip lopsided; only Re(1/Qc) = cos φ / |Qc| is a loss channel.

    Photons in the resonator

    Mean photon number on resonance, from the Q's above and the power at the device, after all the fridge attenuation (see the Fridge Noise Explorer). Resonator loss is usually quoted at about one photon, where two-level-system defects matter most (the next panel shows how much). With an asymmetry φ, only cos φ/|Qc| of the coupling carries energy into the resonator, and that effective coupling is what is used here.

    Two-level-system loss: Qi against power and temperature

    Two-level systems are atomic-scale defects in oxides and interfaces whose level splitting happens to be near hf; each one absorbs a photon and must relax before it can absorb another. At high power there are far more photons than defects, so the TLS saturate and the loss falls as (1 + ⟨n⟩/nc)−β; at high temperature the defects are thermally excited, absorb and emit equally, and the loss falls with the population difference tanh(hf/2kBT). Qubits hold at most one excitation and sit at the cold, single-photon end where this loss is largest, which is why Qi is quoted at ⟨n⟩ ≈ 1 and base temperature: a Qi measured at high power flatters the material.

    1Qi(⟨n⟩, T) = 1QTLS,0 · tanh(hf / 2kBT)(1 + ⟨n⟩/nc)β + 1Qother,   QTLS,0 = 1F tan δ0TLS

    The form used in McRae et al. (2020) and Burnett et al. (2018): QTLS,0 is the TLS limit at zero power and temperature (filling factor F times the intrinsic loss tangent), nc the photon number that saturates the defects, β = ½ for the simplest non-interacting TLS bath (measured devices give 0.1 to 0.5), and Qother everything power-independent (quasiparticles, radiation, package). ⟨n⟩ comes from the photon card above and f from fr; Qother and nc are held constant in T here, so the warm end of the T plot is optimistic (quasiparticle loss appears above about Tc/10).

    How to write a formula

    Use + - * / ^ (or **) and brackets, always with * for multiplication (2*f, not 2f). f is the frequency, i is √−1 and pi is π. Functions: sqrt exp log log10 abs re im conj arg sin cos tan atan sinh cosh tanh. Every other name, like f0, Ql or width, becomes a parameter to fit. Complex formulas are fine: fit them to complex S, or to |S| and the page takes the magnitude.

    Parameters

    Fits need a sensible start. Names like f0, fr, Ql, Qc, w, A, C are recognised.

    The idea in one minute
    
  
    A resonator stores energy at its resonance frequency fr. A quality factor counts how many radians of oscillation it takes
    for the stored energy to fall by a factor e: Q = 2πfr × (energy stored) / (power lost). Energy leaves through two doors:
    
  
      
    
    • Inside: dielectric and surface defects (two-level systems), quasiparticles, radiation, vortices. This
      sets the internal Qi, the number materials and fabrication work tries to raise.
      • 
    
    • Out through the coupling to the line you measure with. This sets the coupling Qc,
      which the designer picks with a coupling capacitor or a gap.
      • 
  
    
  
    Loss rates add, so the inverse Q's add: the measured linewidth gives the loaded Ql,
    
  
    1Ql = 1Qi + 1Qc,
      κ = κi + κc,   κ/2π = fr/Ql (the full width at half maximum).
    
  
    The linewidth alone cannot tell the two doors apart. The depth and shape of the resonance can, because the light leaking out
    through the coupling interferes with the light passing by. That is what the fit uses, and why the phase (the circle in the IQ plane)
    helps so much.

    The models we fit
    
  
    With x = f/fr − 1 and the cable environment a eiαe−2πi f τ
    (attenuation a, phase α, delay τ):
    
  
    Hanger (notch), a resonator side-coupled to a through line:
    
  
    S21 = a eiαe−2πifτ [ 1 − (Ql/|Qc|) eiφ1 + 2i Qlx ]
    
  
    Reflection, a resonator at the end of a line (one port), with twice the coupling term:
    
  
    S11 = a eiαe−2πifτ [ 1 − (2Ql/|Qc|) eiφ1 + 2i Qlx ]
    
  
    Transmission through a resonator between two equal ports (Qc counts both together):
    
  
    S21 = a eiαe−2πifτ Ql/Qc1 + 2i Qlx
    
  
    In every case 1/Qi = 1/Ql − cos φ / |Qc|. The angle φ describes
    an impedance mismatch around the resonator that makes the dip lopsided; taking only the real part of 1/Qc as the coupling loss
    is the "diameter correction method" of Khalil et al. For transmission, the peak height gives Ql/Qc only if |S21| is
    calibrated, so the fit holds a = 1: normalise your data first. These are the forms used by Probst et al. and their
    resonator_tools package, written with this page's sign convention.
    

    


    
  
    Fitting checklist
    
  
      
    
    1. Measure the phase, not only |S|. The IQ circle separates Qi and Qc far better, and in reflection |S11|
      alone cannot tell over- from under-coupled.
      2. 
    
    2. Span 5 to 10 linewidths around the resonance, with at least a few dozen points inside the linewidth.
      3. 
    
    3. Look at the IQ plot. The data should be a clean circle once the delay is out. A spiral means the delay is wrong; a
      squashed circle means the line has a standing wave or the resonator is nonlinear (drive power too high).
      4. 
    
    4. Check the residuals. They should look like noise with no structure and about the size of the point-to-point scatter.
      5. 
    
    5. Report what you measured: power or photon number, temperature, the model and whether φ was free, and Qi with its
      uncertainty. Qi usually rises with power as two-level systems saturate, so quote the single-photon value for loss studies.
      6. 
    
    6. Be wary far from critical coupling. If Qi ≫ Qc, the error bar on Qi grows quickly; redesign
      the coupling if you need Qi precisely.
      7.

    How the fit works
    
  
    Starting values. The cable delay is first estimated from the phase slope at both edges, then refined to the delay that puts the
    data most nearly on a circle. The background (a, α) comes from the edges, fr from the strongest response,
    Ql from the half-power width, Qc from the depth and φ from the angle of the resonance.
    
  
    Least squares. A Levenberg–Marquardt fit then minimises the sum of squared differences, over the real and imaginary parts
    when the data have phase. It first fits the resonance with the background held, then everything together. Ql, Qc
    and a are fitted as logarithms so they stay positive. A weakly coupled resonator makes a small circle, and the delay can lock
    in a whole turn off, so the fit also tries starting delays a turn either side and keeps the best.
    
  
    Uncertainties. The covariance is s²(JTJ)−1, where J is the Jacobian at the
    best fit and s² the residual variance, the usual estimate when the noise level is not given. Derived quantities such as
    Qi carry the full covariance, correlations included. On simulated data, the true value falls inside the ±1σ range about 68%
    of the time, as it should; tests in the repository check this.
    
  
    Your own formulas are parsed, never run as code, and evaluated with complex numbers wherever they appear.
