🧹 ErrorSweeper find the errors you cannot see

Quantum error correction as a deduction game: you never look at the data qubits, only at check qubits (ancillas) that light up when an odd number of their neighbours has an error. Read the lamps, fix the errors, and don't stretch your fix across the code.

More about the game and the physics

Measuring a data qubit would destroy the quantum state, so check qubits measure the parity of a few neighbours instead. When every lamp is off, the truth is revealed: if your correction and the errors form a closed loop, the logical qubit survived; if they stretch across the code, you just flipped the logical qubit. The physics is real: these are the rotated surface code, the toric code, and the Steane and Shor codes, under code-capacity noise.

Who checks the checker? (fault tolerance)

The voting circuit can fail too. Girvin calls this the “who watches the watchman?” problem: a single vote is a single point of failure, so a fault-tolerant design repeats the checks and votes on the voters, and the gain survives as long as each part fails rarely enough. Real quantum codes face the same problem, which is why their checks are measured over and over (chapter 4 leaves this out).

    Design challenges

      Real codes were found this way: someone drew a new arrangement of checks and measured it. The rule to beat is the surface code’s k·d²/n = 1. Flat codes with local checks can only do a constant factor better (Bravyi, Poulin & Terhal 2010); color codes get closer to the limit with bigger faces. Share your best design with the link button.

      Missions

      Each mission is a small piece of a real research question: find codes that protect more information (k), against more errors (d), with fewer qubits (n), under a hardware rule. The engine scores every design exactly, and the best designs from all players form a shared map of what is possible.

      How a stencil makes a code

      The qubits sit on an ℓ × m torus, two per cell: a left one (grid A) and a right one (grid B). Every cell also holds one X check and one Z check. The X check at cell c touches the left qubits at c + (each gold cell) and the right qubits at c + (each blue cell), with positions wrapping around the torus; the Z check uses the mirror image. Shifting one stencil everywhere keeps every X check commuting with every Z check, so every stencil gives a valid quantum code, and the engine finds how many logical qubits (k) it stores and its distance (d). These are the bivariate bicycle codes of Bravyi et al. (2024), found by exactly this kind of search; with mixed steps xayb they are the abelian two-block codes.

      Distances are exact when the search can prove them (up to about 150 qubits); otherwise the page shows an upper bound “d ≤” from random information sets (the QDistRnd method), which can only overstate d, never understate it.

        Where real qubits are

        A gate with fidelity F makes an error with probability about 1 − F, so a 99.5% two-qubit gate is p ≈ 0.5%, under the 1% circuit-noise threshold but not far under it. Λ is what a real experiment measures: how many times the logical error rate drops for each step in distance, d → d + 2. Λ > 1 means the device is below threshold; the curves above give Λ ≈ (pth/p) for codes like these.

        The physics

        Why lamps, not looking

        Measuring a data qubit would collapse the superposition the code protects. A stabilizer measurement only asks “is the parity of these qubits even?”, which every valid code state answers the same way, so it reveals errors without revealing the data. A Z⊗Z⊗Z⊗Z check flips sign when an odd number of its qubits suffered an X (bit-flip) error; an X⊗X⊗X⊗X check does the same for Z (phase-flip) errors; a Y error is both. That pattern of flipped checks is the syndrome.

        Why loops are harmless and strings are fatal

        After your correction the lamps are off, so errors plus corrections form chains with no loose ends. A closed chain that encloses a region (one you could shrink to nothing), or a chain that leaves an edge and comes back to the same edge, is a product of checks (a stabilizer) and does nothing to the encoded qubit, which is why many different corrections can all be right (degeneracy). A chain that runs from one edge of the code to the opposite edge, or around the torus, is a logical operator: it flips the encoded qubit and no lamp can see it. The code distance d is the length of the shortest such chain, so a code can always fix up to ⌊(d − 1)/2⌋ errors.

        What this game leaves out

        Code-capacity noise: each round the checks are measured once and perfectly. Real hardware repeats the measurements many times, because the ancillas and the measurements make errors too, and decodes in space and time; that lowers the surface-code threshold to about 1%. The decoder looks for the lightest set of errors that explains the lamps. For the surface, toric and repetition codes it does this by exact minimum-weight matching (greedy pairing when more than 16 lamps of one type are lit, which is rare here); for codes without a matching graph, such as the Steane code and many lab designs, it uses an exact lookup table or search. It decodes X and Z separately, so for Y errors it ignores their correlation.

        How matching works, and when it cannot

        An error on a qubit in the middle of a surface code lights the two lamps of its kind on either side; an error at the edge lights one. So the lit lamps are the ends of error chains, and decoding means pairing them up (or pairing one with the edge) so that the chains joining them are as short as possible: a minimum-weight perfect matching on the graph whose nodes are lamps and whose edges are qubits (Dennis et al. 2002; Edmonds’ blossom algorithm solves it in polynomial time, and sparse-blossom implementations decode real experiments in real time). This only works when every qubit touches at most two lamps of each kind. In a color code a qubit touches three, one error lights three lamps, and the problem becomes a hypergraph; decoders then use other ideas, such as projecting onto matchable sub-lattices or belief propagation with ordered-statistics decoding (Roffe et al. 2020). Turn on Show the matching graph in the lab’s Test mode to see the graph for your own code.

        Open questions this page cannot settle

        Everything here is code-capacity noise: perfect, one-shot checks. Real thresholds come from circuit-level noise, where the check circuits themselves fail, errors spread through the CNOTs (the order of the four gates matters: “hook” errors), and decoding runs in space and time; the surface code's threshold falls from about 10% to about 1%. Color codes and most lab designs have no matching graph, so which decoder, and at what speed? And for qLDPC codes like the ones in Code Hunt, how do you lay out long-range checks on a chip? Export your lab code's check matrices (Code lab → Export H) to try your own decoder, for example with the open-source ldpc or Stim packages.
