Your qubit's height is its polar angle θ on the Bloch sphere, drawn with the ground state |0⟩ at the bottom and the excited state |1⟩ at the top, so height is energy, with P(1) = sin²(θ/2) in between. The course scrolls past in time. The drive is exactly on resonance, so nothing precesses: hold ▲ to turn the qubit toward |1⟩ and ▼ toward |0⟩. Let go and only T₁ decay acts, and it always pulls the qubit down to |0⟩, like gravity. A shorter T₁ is a harder game. The physics

In the frame rotating with a drive that is exactly on resonance there is no free precession. A drive of phase 0 (+X) or π (−X) gives

H = ±(ħΩ/2) σx,  dr/dt = ω × r,  ω = (±Ω, 0, 0),  P(1) = sin²(θ/2)

so the Bloch vector turns about the x axis at the Rabi rate Ω (here 1.6 rad/s: a π pulse, |0⟩ → |1⟩, takes about 2 s; on a transmon it typically takes 10–50 nanoseconds). A turn about x keeps the state on the circle through |0⟩ and |1⟩ in the y–z plane, so its height θ says everything, which is why the game can draw it as a line. It is drawn with |0⟩ at the bottom (the usual Bloch sphere puts it at the top; the sphere on the right is the usual one turned upside down about x), so height is energy and decay falls. ▲ and ▼ pick the drive's sign that turns θ the way you ask, and the drive eases off as the state reaches |0⟩ or |1⟩. A constant drive would carry the state through |1⟩ and back again, P(1) = sin²(Ωt/2), a Rabi oscillation; the game stops it at the poles and reverses the drive when you ask. The phase in the ket, cos(θ/2)|0⟩ ∓ i sin(θ/2)|1⟩, is the −i that a turn about x leaves; it does not change P(1), but a turn about y or z would feel it (see Qubit Copter).

Measurement gates are projective measurements of σz: the state jumps to |1⟩ with probability sin²(θ/2) and to |0⟩ otherwise (the Born rule). X tokens apply the X gate, a π turn about x, so θ → 180° − θ. Noise clouds are stray coupling to the environment, which acts as a measurement nobody asked for: touching one collapses the state to |1⟩ with probability sin²(θ/2) and to |0⟩ otherwise, exactly like a measurement gate. That is decoherence: the environment learns the state, and the superposition is gone. T₁ decay is always on, as it is for a real qubit: it emits a photon and falls to its ground state. The qubit here is a watched quantum trajectory, γ = 1/T₁: between photon detections it slides toward |0⟩ at dθ/dt = −(γ/2) sin θ, and it jumps to |0⟩ at the rate γ·P(1), most often near |1⟩; with the drive off, averaging many runs gives exponential decay, e−t/T₁. The difficulty is T₁: 20, 10, 5 or 2.5 s (6 s in the Quantum jumps mission), only a few π pulses long so you can see it happen; a transmon's T₁ of about 100 µs lasts thousands of 20 ns π pulses. Keeping a qubit excited means fighting decay, which is why fast gates matter.

Left out, on purpose: pure dephasing (random turns about z, the Tφ part of T₂), so here T₂ = 2T₁, the most a qubit can have; detuning, so nothing precesses (see Qubit Copter's Z control for that); and readout imperfections: every measurement here is instantaneous and never wrong. The Virtual Qubit Lab adds detuning, decay during the pulses and the shot noise of a finite number of measurements.
